Based on the provided document segment from *"The Future of Computational Law" (2024)*, here are the key insights and facts organized by theme:

### 1. Challenges in Evaluating Large Language Models (LLMs)
*   **Lack of Real-World Context:** Current evaluations of LLMs are insufficient because they lack "naturalistic datasets." Because there is a lack of transparency regarding how people use LLMs daily, researchers cannot accurately measure how often models provide useful answers versus irrelevant or inaccurate ones.
*   **The Need for Naturalistic Evaluation:** To improve "construct validity," researchers should conduct studies observing how people actually use LLMs in real-world settings, rather than relying solely on human-style exam benchmarks (like the Bar exam).
*   **Data Contamination:** Current AI evaluations suffer from "contamination," where the training data overlaps with the evaluation data, leading to inflated and unrealistic performance estimates.

### 2. Risks of Generative AI in Legal Practice
*   **Hallucinations and Sanctions:** Language models can fabricate information with high authority. This has led to real-world consequences, including lawyers being sanctioned by courts for including fabricated information in legal filings.
*   **Inadequate Disclaimers:** While companies like OpenAI and Anthropic have implemented disclaimers (e.g., "ChatGPT can make mistakes"), these are often insufficient to prevent professional damage. In response, some judges are issuing "chambers' rules" to dictate how lawyers must disclose AI use.

### 3. Opportunities for "Narrow" AI Applications
*   **High-Observability Use Cases:** The paper suggests that AI is most effective in "narrow settings" with well-defined outcomes and high observability. 
*   **Example - Error Checking:** A successful application is checking for errors in legal documents (e.g., the Social Security Administration uses a model to ensure adjudicators address all medical claims in denial decisions).
*   **Potential for Patent Law:** Since 86% of patent applications receive at least one rejection, semi-automated error-checking could significantly reduce costs for both applicants and the USPTO.
*   **Constraint for Safety:** The authors argue that such AI should provide "helpful, informative recommendations" rather than acting as "binding mechanisms," and must always be accompanied by a thorough appeals process.

### 4. Failures of "Predictive AI" (Predicting Outcomes and People)
*   **Flawed Research in Outcome Prediction:** A review of 171 papers claiming to predict court outcomes found that only 12 (7%) actually performed the task without "data leakage." Many studies "cheated" by using the final judgment text (which already contains the verdict) to predict the verdict.
*   **Bias and Inaccuracy in Human-Centric AI:**
    *   **COMPAS:** An investigation into the COMPAS algorithm (used for recidivism prediction) found it had twice as many false positives for Black defendants as White defendants, and its overall accuracy (65%) was no better than predictions made by non-experts.
    *   **Distribution Shift:** Tools like the Public Safety Assessment (PSA) fail when applied to specific regions because they are trained on nationwide data that does not reflect local crime patterns (e.g., Cook County, Illinois).
*   **Lack of Contestability:** Predictive AI vendors often protect their algorithms as "trade secrets," making it impossible for the subjects of these decisions (e.g., defendants) to contest the logic used against them.

### 5. Conclusion: The Shift to Socio-Technical Assessment
*   **The Core Thesis:** The authors argue that the effective deployment of AI in law requires a shift from purely **technical evaluations** to **socio-technical assessments**. 
*   **Evaluating Impact:** Evaluations must move beyond technical specifications to consider the broader societal impact, transparency, and the ability for individuals to contest automated decisions.
