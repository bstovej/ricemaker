### **Lecture 9: The Shrinking Warrant Requirement**
The central theme of this section is that while the Fourth Amendment generally protects against unreasonable searches and seizures, the "warrant requirement" is increasingly bypassed by various legal exceptions.

**Key Legal Facts & Exceptions:**
*   **Plain View Doctrine:** Police may seize evidence without a warrant if they are legally present in a location and the evidence is clearly visible (e.g., drugs on a table in a home where an officer was invited).
*   **Exigent Circumstances:** Warrants are not required if an emergency exists, such as the imminent destruction of evidence (e.g., flushing drugs down a toilet).
*   **The Automobile Exception:** Because cars are mobile and have a "lower expectation of privacy" (due to windows and accident risks), police only need **probable cause** to search a vehicle, including its containers (trunk, glove box, etc.).
*   **Inventory Searches:** If a car is impounded, police may search it without probable cause to ensure all property is accounted for and to prevent theft.

*   **Search Incident to Arrest:** Upon arrest, police can search the person and the immediate area within their reach to check for weapons or evidence.
*   **Terry Stops (*Terry v. Ohio*):** Police may stop and "frisk" a person based on **reasonable suspicion** (a lower standard than probable cause) if they suspect criminal activity or that a person is armed and dangerous.
*   **Special Needs Doctrine:** Searches can be conducted without probable cause if they serve a "special governmental need" unrelated to crime control (e.g., school locker searches, drug testing pilots, or border searches).
*   **Consent:** A search is entirely legal if the individual provides voluntary, non-coerced consent.

### **Lecture 10: The Fifth Amendment Privilege**
This section explores the protection against **compelled self-incrimination**, a right rooted in the need to prevent government abuses like the historical English trials for heresy and sedition.

**The Four Requirements for the Privilege to Apply:**
1.  **Compulsion:** The individual must be under compulsion (e.g., being in custody or facing legal penalties for silence).
2.  **Incrimination:** The testimony must be self-incriminating.
3.  **Fifth Amendment Protection:** The person must be invoking their constitutional right.
4.  **Testimony:** The communication must be "testimonial" (not just physical evidence).

**Key Legal Principles:**
*   **Scope of Protection:** The privilege does not protect against all silence; it specifically protects against being forced to provide self-incriminating testimony.
*   **Limitations:** The privilege does not necessarily apply to "physical" evidence, and it does not prevent the government from compelling testimony in non-criminal contexts (like certain regulatory settings).
*   **Corporations:** Unlike individuals, corporations generally do not have a Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination.

### **Lecture 11: Miranda and the Right to Counsel**
This section focuses on the evolution of protections during police interrogations.

**Key Legal Concepts:**
*   **The Evolution of Voluntariness:** Historically, courts looked at whether a confession was "voluntary" under the Due Process Clause (focusing on whether police coercion/physical force was used). 
*   **The Shift to Procedural Safeguards:** The *Miranda* framework moved the focus from the "voluntariness" of the confession to the **adequacy of the warnings** provided. The goal was to ensure that the suspect's waiver of rights was knowing, intelligent, and voluntary.
*   **The Purpose of Warnings:** The primary purpose of the warnings (the right to remain silent and the right to an attorney) is to counteract the inherently coercive nature of police custodial interrogation.
*   **Invocation of Rights:** For the protections to be triggered, the suspect must clearly and unambiguously invoke their right to remain silent or their right to counsel.
