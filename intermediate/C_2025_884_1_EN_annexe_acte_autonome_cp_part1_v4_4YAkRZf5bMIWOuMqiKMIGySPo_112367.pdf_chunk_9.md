This document segment provides a detailed legal interpretation of **Article 5(1)(c) of the EU AI Act**, which prohibits certain types of AI-enabled "social scoring." The text outlines the boundaries between prohibited practices and lawful, legitimate use of AI for evaluation.

Below are the key insights and facts categorized by theme.

### 1. The Core Prohibition: AI Social Scoring
The fundamental rule is that AI-enabled social scoring is prohibited when the score leads to **detrimental or unfavourable treatment** under specific conditions.

*   **Causality:** For the prohibition to apply, there must be a causal link where the score is the cause of the treatment.
*   **Not Necessarily the Sole Cause:** An AI system does not have to be the *only* factor in a decision. If an AI score plays a "sufficiently important role" in a decision—even if combined with human assessment—it falls under the prohibition.
*   **Scope of Actors:** The prohibition applies to both **public authorities** (e.g., tax agencies) and **private entities** (e.g., insurance companies or credit agencies).
*   **Temporal Scope:** The prohibition covers the "placing on the market" of such systems, meaning the ban applies even if the harmful consequences have not yet materialized.

### 2. The Two Scenarios for Prohibited Treatment
The document identifies two distinct "scenarios" that trigger the prohibition. A practice is prohibited if it meets **at least one** of these criteria:

#### **Scenario A: Unrelated Social Contexts**
The use of data to score a person in a context that has no apparent connection to where the data was originally collected.
*   **Example:** A tax authority using a person's social media habits or internet connections to decide whether to audit their tax return.
*   **Example:** A welfare agency using a person's nationality or workplace performance to estimate the probability of benefit fraud.

#### **Scenario B: Unjustified or Disproportionate Treatment**
The treatment resulting from the score is far more severe than the actual behavior warrants.
*   **Example:** A municipality using "minor misbehavior" (like not returning a library book on time) as a basis for "blacklisting" a resident from public benefits.
*   **Example:** Removing children from a family because parents committed minor infractions like receiving a traffic fine.

### 3. Key Legal Distinctions
The text provides precise definitions to help distinguish between different types of impact:

*   **"Unfavourable" vs. "Detrimental" Treatment:**
    *   **Unfavourable:** Being treated less favourably than others (e.g., being singled out for extra inspections). This does not require actual physical or financial harm.
    *   **Detrimental:** Requiring the person to suffer actual harm or damage (e.s., significant financial hardship).
*   **Discrimination:** While the prohibited practice may be discriminatory, it does **not** have to violate EU non-discrimination law to be prohibited under the AI Act. The AI Act can cover "unfair" treatment that falls outside protected categories like race or religion.
*   **Legal Entities vs. Natural Persons:** The prohibition applies to the scoring of **natural persons** (human beings). Scoring companies (legal entities) is generally out of scope, *unless* that score is an aggregate of individual behaviors that directly impacts the humans involved (e.g., scoring all employees in a specific company).

### 4. The "Safe Harbor": What is Lawful?
The document emphasizes that AI-based evaluation is **not** per se prohibited. Scoring is lawful if it meets the following criteria:
1.  **Legitimate Purpose:** It is for a specific, lawful purpose (e.g., fraud prevention, creditworthiness).
2.  **Data Relevance:** The data used is relevant to the purpose and was collected in a related context.
3.  **Legal Compliance:** It complies with existing Union and national laws (e.g., consumer protection or banking laws).
4.  **Proportionality:** The resulting treatment is justified and proportionate to the behavior being assessed.

**Examples of Lawful Practices:**
*   **Credit Scoring:** Using income and expense data to determine loan eligibility.
*   **Fraud Detection:** Using transaction metadata to identify fraudulent activity.
*   **Telematics:** Using driving speed and braking data to adjust auto insurance premiums.
