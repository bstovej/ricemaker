## 📊 I. Statistical & Quantitative Analysis Facts

### A. Correlation and Causality (The most critical concepts)
*   **Correlation $\neq$ Causation:** Simply finding a correlation is insufficient to prove a causal relationship.
*   **Requirements for Causality:** To confidently establish causation, one must demonstrate:
    1.  Correlation.
    2.  Temporal Order (the cause must precede the effect).
    3.  A Theoretical Explanation.
    4.  Elimination of alternative explanations (confounding variables).
*   **Pearson’s $r$:** This is the specific calculation used to determine the strength of the linear correlation between two **ratio-level** variables.

### B. Distributions and Data Points
*   **Normal Distribution:** A key characteristic of a normal distribution is that the **mean, median, and mode are equal**.
*   **Z-Score:** A high absolute z-score (e.g., $\pm 3.87$) indicates an **outlier**, also described as a **deviant or extreme case**.
*   **Confidence Intervals:** When analyzing polling results, the **margin of error** (e.g., $\pm 3\%$) and the **confidence level** (e.g., 95%) define a *range* of probability for the population estimate.

### C. Levels of Measurement
Understanding the type of variable is foundational for choosing the correct statistical test:
*   **Nominal:** Categories only (e.g., dog breed). No inherent order.
*   **Ordinal:** Categories with a ranking or order (e.g., satisfaction rating: Very Satisfied $\rightarrow$ Not at All Satisfied).
*   **Interval:** Ordered variables where the distance between points is meaningful, but there is **no true zero** (e.g., years graduated).
*   **Ratio:** Ordered variables with meaningful distance and a **true zero** (zero means the complete absence of the quantity, e.g., number of books).

## 🔬 II. Research Design & Methodology Insights

### A. Research Scope and Approach
*   **Quantitative vs. Qualitative:** Generally, **quantitative analysis** is performed with a large sample size ($n$), while **qualitative analysis** is preferred when the sample size is small.
*   **Unit of Analysis:** This is the **ordering unit** used for data collection (e.g., is the data gathered at the level of the individual, the family, or the country?).
*   **Action Research:** This research model aims at **solving a specific local problem** by working directly *with* the community, rather than building general theory.
*   **Mixed Methods:** The process of combining both quantitative (numerical) and qualitative (descriptive) techniques.

### B. Data Collection Techniques
*   **Focus Groups:** Ideal for gathering insights not only from individuals but also from the **dynamics and conversation** *between* group members.
*   **Sampling:** A **random sample** requires that every member of the population has an equal chance of being selected.
*   **Threats to Validity:** Researchers must account for potential biases, such as **Mortality Effects** (the loss of subjects from the study over time).

### C. Writing and Hypothesis Development
*   **Literature Review:** This is both a **process** (the continuous finding and evaluating of scholarly work) and a **section** of the report (summarizing prior findings, gaps, and debates).
*   **Qualitative Saturation:** Data collection should stop when further data or analysis no longer yields additional unique insights.
*   **Hypothesis Testing:** Hypotheses must be specific, directional, and logical. Avoid connecting variables where the relationship is difficult or impossible to define directionally (e.g., wall color and mood).

## 💡 III. Ethical, Philosophical, and Scholarly Practices

### A. Ethical Principles (The Belmont Report)
The three fundamental principles guiding ethical research are:
1.  **Respect for Persons:** Recognizing the autonomy and dignity of participants.
2.  **Beneficence:** Ensuring the research maximizes potential benefits while minimizing harm.
3.  **Justice:** Ensuring that the risks and benefits are distributed fairly across populations.

### B. Types of Inquiry
*   **Nomothetic vs. Idiographic:** (Implied context) A distinction between studying general laws/patterns vs. studying unique individuals.
*   **Observation/Ethics:** (Implied context) The ethical imperative to maintain neutrality and rigorous documentation in study design.

### C. Source Credibility
*   **Academic Sources:** The necessity of citing peer-reviewed literature to substantiate claims, reinforcing methodological rigor.

### D. Data Collection Techniques
*   **Surveys:** Emphasizing the construction of non-leading and unambiguous survey questions.
*   **Interviews:** Adherence to established protocols to ensure standardized data capture.
