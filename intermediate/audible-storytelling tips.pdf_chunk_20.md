### **I. Point of View (Narrative Voice)**
The text explores how the choice of narrator affects the reader's relationship with the story:

*   **The Role of the Narrator:** The narrator’s "voice" can range from objective to highly judgmental.
*   **First-Person/Limited Perspective:** Using a narrator with a close, restricted view (as seen in the "witness" or "protagonist" roles) allows for intimacy but limits the scope of information.
*   **The Omniscient Narrator:** A narrator who possesses god-like knowledge, able to move between characters' thoughts and provide historical context.
*   **The Authoritative/Judgmental Narrator:**
    *   **Opinionated:** Some narrators (like those in Dickens) can be overtly judgmental, offering moral commentary on characters.

### **II. The Function of Setting**
The text argues that setting is much more than a mere backdrop; it is a dynamic tool used for several narrative purposes:

*   **Setting as Metaphor/Symbolism:**
    *   **Reflecting Theme:** A setting can mirror the internal state of a character or the central conflict of the book.
    *   **Setting as Metaphor:** The text describes how a setting can act as a metaphor for the characters' struggles (e.g., a decaying house mirroring a decaying soul).
*   **Setting as Mood and Atmosphere (Atmospheric Setting):**
    *   **Establishing Tone:** Settings are used to evoke specific emotions like dread, peace, or nostalgia (e.g., using "fog" or "shadows" to create suspense).
    *   **Creating Mood:** The sensory details of a setting (sight, sound, smell) are used to manipulate the reader's emotional response.
*   **Setting as Character (Characterization):**
    *   **Reflecting Character Traits:** A character’s environment—their home, their room, the cleanliness of their workspace—can reveal their personality, social status, or mental state without the author having to state it explicitly.
    *   **Externalizing Internal Conflict:** The physical landscape can represent a character's internal psychological struggle.
*   **Setting as Plot Driver (Structural Setting):**
    *   **Catalyst for Action:** The physical limitations or features of a setting (e.g., a locked room, a treacherous mountain pass) can force characters to act or create obstacles that drive the plot forward.
    *   **Providing Context/World-Building:** The setting establishes the "rules" of the story's world (historical period, social hierarchies, or magical laws).
