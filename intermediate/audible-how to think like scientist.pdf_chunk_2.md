This document consists of lecture notes (Lectures 2 and 3) regarding the principles of **skepticism, the scientific method, and logical fallacies**. The text focuses on providing a toolkit for distinguishing between empirical science and pseudoscience.

The following are the key insights and facts organized by theme:

### 1. The Nature of Science and Skepticism
*   **Definition of Science:** Science is a set of methods used to describe and interpret observed or inferred phenomena. It relies on **objectivity** (external validation) rather than subjectivity.
*   **The Scientific Method Components:**
    *   **Induction/Deduction:** Moving from observations to theories and testing them.
    able to use **Observation**, **Hypothesis**, and **Testing**.
    *   **Falsifiability:** A core concept is that scientific claims must be testable and potentially disprovable.
*   **Scientific Pillars:**
    *   **Objectivity vs. Subjectivity:** Science seeks to move away from personal bias toward evidence-based conclusions.
    *   **Provisional Nature:** Scientific "truths" are always subject to revision based on new evidence.
*   **Key Terminology:**
    *   **Theory:** A well-substantiated explanation of some aspect of the natural world.
    *   **Construct/Theory vs. Fact:** The text distinguishes between the "laws" of nature and the "theories" used to explain them.

### 2. The Demarcation Problem (Science vs. Pseudoscience)
The text explores how to distinguish between scientific inquiry and "pseudoscience" through several lenses:
*   **The Demarcation Criterion:** A primary way to identify science is through **falsifiability**—the ability for a claim to be proven wrong.
*   **The Three Categories of Knowledge:**
    1.  **Science:** Uses empirical evidence and is subject to change.
    2.  **Pseudoscience:** Mimics scientific language but lacks the ability to be tested or corrected.
    3.  **Non-science (e.g., Faith/Philosophy):** Areas that may be meaningful but do not rely on empirical testing.
*   **The Role of Utility:** Science is characterized by its ability to create predictive models and utility (e.g., medicine, technology).

### 3. Logic, Fallacies, and Cognitive Biases
The lecture identifies several logical errors and "traps" that undermine rational thought:
*   **Logical Fallacies:**
    *   **Ad Hominem:** Attacking the person making the argument rather than the argument itself.
    *   **Argument from Ignorance:** Assuming a claim is true simply because it hasn't been proven false.
    *   **Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc:** The fallacy of assuming that because event B followed event A, event A *caused* event B.
    *   **False Dilemma (Black-and-White Fallacy):** Presenting only two options when more exist.
*   **Cognitive Biases:**
    *   **Confirmation Bias:** The tendency to search for, interpret, and recall information that confirms one's pre-existing beliefs.
    *   **Base Rate Neglect:** Focusing on specific information while ignoring the broader statistical context (e.g., ignoring the actual frequency of an event in favor of a "sensational" anecdote).

### 4. Statistical and Probability Concepts
The text touches on the importance of understanding probability to avoid being misled by "anecdotal evidence":
*   **The Law of Large Numbers:** Emphasizes that in large samples, the average of results should be close to the expected value.
*   **The Importance of Sample Size:** Small samples (anecdotes) are prone to extreme fluctuations and do not represent the "true" reality as well as large, controlled samples.
*   **Probability vs. Certainty:** Science deals in probabilities and degrees of certainty rather than absolute, unchangeable truths.

### 5. Key Philosophical Principles for Skepticism
*   **Hume’s Problem of Induction:** The realization that just because something happened in the past does not *guarantee* it will happen in the future (the basis for scientific skepticism).
*   **Occam’s Razor:** The principle that, when presented with competing hypotheses, the one with the fewest assumptions (the simplest) is usually the best.
*   **Falsifiability (Popper):** The idea that for a theory to be scientific, it must be able to be refuted by observation.
