### **Part 1: Relitigation and Preclusion**

This section covers the rules that prevent parties from reopening legal disputes that have already been decided.

#### **1. Claim Preclusion (Res Judicata)**
*   **The Basic Rule:** Once a claim has been adjudicated on the merits, it cannot be relitigated in a subsequent lawsuit.
*   **Scope of "The Same Claim":** A claim is considered the "same" if it arises from the same set of circumstances or is logically related. 
    *   *Example:* If you are in a car accident, you cannot sue for a broken arm in one suit and a broken leg in a second suit; both must be brought at once.
*   **Defense Preclusion:** The rule also applies to defendants; they must assert all available defenses in the initial suit to prevent them from being raised later.
*   **Justifications:** 
    *   **Efficiency:** Reduces the total number of lawsuits and saves judicial resources.
    *   **Equity:** Prevents "serial litigation" and the harassment of defendants.
    *   **Finality:** Promotes "repose" (the idea that legal disputes should eventually end).

#### **2. Issue Preclusion (Non-Mutual Collateral Estoppel)**
*   Unlike claim preclusion, which prevents re-litigating an entire case, this prevents re-litigating specific *issues* that were already decided.

#### ** 3. Non-Mutual Preclusion (Offensive vs. Defensive)**
*   **Defensive:** A new defendant uses a previous judgment to prevent a plaintiff from bringing a new claim against them on the same issue.
*   **Offensive:** A new plaintiff uses a previous judgment to prevent a defendant from re-litigating an issue they previously lost.
*   **The Rule on "Non-Mutual" Preclusion:** Courts generally allow some degree of non-mutual preclusion, but it is subject to fairness. Specifically, regarding **Offensive Use**, courts look at whether the new plaintiff is "opportunistic" (e.g., waiting to see if someone else loses on an issue before suing).

### **Part 2: Standards of Appellate Review**

The text outlines how much "deference" an appellate court gives to a lower court’s decision.

#### **1. Standards of Review**
*   **Questions of Fact:** Appellate courts use a highly deferential standard. They will only overturn a trial court's finding of fact if it is clearly erroneous. They defer to the trial judge because the trial judge actually witnessed the witnesses and evidence.
*   **Questions of Law:** Appellate courts use a **de novo** standard (meaning "anew"). They do not defer to the trial court. They examine the legal issue from scratch to ensure the law is applied uniformly across all courts.

#### **2. The Role of the Appellate Court**
*   The appellate court's primary function is to ensure the correct application of the law and to maintain consistency in the legal system. It is not a "second trial" for the facts; it is a review of the legal errors made during the first trial.
