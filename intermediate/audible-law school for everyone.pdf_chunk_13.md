### 1. Procedural Due Process: *Goldberg* vs. *Mathews*
The text uses two landmark Supreme Court cases to illustrate how courts balance individual rights against government interests when determining the required level of procedural fairness.

*   **The *Goldberg* Standard (Welfare Benefits):**
    *   **Rule:** The state must provide an extensive hearing **before** terminating benefits.
    *   **Reasoning:** The loss of welfare benefits can be "catastaryphic" (loss of food/shelter). Therefore, the individual’s interest in uninterrupted benefits outweighs the state's interest in saving resources.
*   **The *Mathews* Standard (Disability Benefits):**
    *   **Rule:** The government is **not** required to provide a full hearing before termination; a hearing to contest the decision *after* termination is sufficient.
    *   **Reasoning:** Disability benefits are based on status, not financial need, making termination less "traumatic." Additionally, the administrative burden of providing upfront hearings for millions of recipients would cost taxpayers billions of dollars.
*   **Key Insight:** Legal reasoning in procedure involves a "balancing test" of competing interests (individual hardship vs. state efficiency).

### 2. Subject-Matter Jurisdiction (SMJ)
Subject-matter jurisdiction refers to a court's authority to hear cases involving a particular topic or type of dispute.

*   **Federal vs. State Systems:**
    *   **State Courts:** Often have specialized courts (e.g., family law) to ensure efficiency.
    *   **Federal Courts:** Their power is limited by **Article III of the Constitution**. Federal courts cannot hear just any case; they must have specific authority.
*   **Two Primary Bases for Federal Jurisdiction:**
    1.  **Federal-Question Jurisdiction:** The claim must "arise under" federal law (e.g., a right created by a federal statute or the Constitution).
        *   *Crucial Distinction:* Simply using a federal standard to prove a state-law claim (like negligence) or raising federal law as a **defense** does **not** create federal-question jurisdiction. The plaintiff's actual claim must be federal.
    2.  **Diversity Jurisdiction:** Allows federal courts to hear cases between citizens of different states.
        *   **Requirements:** There must be "diversity of citizenship" and the amount in controversy must exceed a certain threshold (implied).
        *   **Domicile:** Citizenship is determined by "domicile" (where a person intends to remain).
*   **Diversity of Citizenship Nuance:** The amount in controversy is often determined by the plaintiff's claim.

### 3. Personal Jurisdiction (Jurisdiction over the Defendant)
This refers to a court's power to adjudicate a case against a specific defendant.

*   **The Concept of Service:** For a court to exercise power, the defendant must be properly served with process (notice of the lawsuit).
*   **Historical Evolution (The "Pennoyer" Era):**
    *   **Traditional View:** A court's power was strictly tied to physical presence or property within the state.
    *   **The "Pennoyer" Approach (via *Pennoyer v. Neff* logic):** In the past, a defendant had to be physically served within the state or have property there to be subject to the court's power.
*   **Evolution of Service via Statute/Agency:**
    *   **Hess/Agency Theory:** As society became more mobile (automobiles), the law evolved to allow service via agents (e.g., serving a driver via the Secretary of State) to ensure defendants could not evade lawsuits simply by traveling.
