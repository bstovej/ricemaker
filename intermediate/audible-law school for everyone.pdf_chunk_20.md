This document comprises two lecture segments from a Torts law course. It explores the two distinct components of causation in negligence: **Factual Causation** (the scientific/empirical inquiry) and **Legal Causation** (the policy/moral inquiry).

Below are the key insights and facts organized by topic.

### 1. Factual Causation (The "But-For" Test)
Factual causation focuses on whether the defendant's actions were the actual cause of the injury in a physical sense.

*   **The Core Question:** Did the injury occur because of the defendant's actions? (i.e., "But for the defendant's conduct, would the injury have happened?")
*   **The Challenge of Uncertainty:** In complex cases, determining factual causation can be difficult.
*   **Indeterminate Causes:** The text discusses "indeterminate causes," where multiple forces contribute to an injury. 
    *   **Example:** If two fires merge to burn a house, it is difficult to prove which fire caused the damage.
    *   **Legal Rule (The "All-or-Nothing" Approach):** In cases where it is impossible to determine which specific cause was responsible (e.**g., two fires**), the law often holds all contributors liable to prevent injustice, provided their contribution was a substantial factor.

### 2. Legal Causation (Proximate Cause)
Even if factual causation is proven, the law limits liability through "proximate cause" to prevent infinite liability. This is a policy-based decision to determine where responsibility ends.

*   **The Core Question:** Is it fair to hold the defendant liable, or was the connection between the act and the injury too remote?
*   **Key Concept: Foreseeability:** The primary test for legal causation is whether the harm was a **foreseeable consequence** of the defendant’s actions.
*   **Intervening Superseding Causes:** A new, intervening event may "break the chain" of causation. If a subsequent event is so unforeseeable or extraordinary that it becomes the true cause of the injury, the original defendant is relieved of liability.

### 3. Key Legal Doctrines and Case Examples
The text uses several landmark legal scenarios to illustrate these concepts:

#### **A. Foreseeability and the "Eggshell Skull" Rule**
*   **The Doctrine:** While the *type* of harm must be foreseeable, the *extent* of the harm does not have to be. This is often referred to as the "Eggshell Skull" rule—you take your victim as you find them.
*   **Application:** If you negligently hit someone and they happen to have an unusually fragile skull that shatters, you are liable for the full extent of the injury, even though the extreme severity was unforeseeable.

#### **B. The "Eggshell Skull" vs. "Eggshell Mind" (The Limit of Liability)**
*   **The Doctrine:** The law distinguishes between the physical extent of injury and the "type" of injury. 
*   **Example (Wagon Mound Case/Logic):** If the *type* of injury (e.g., physical injury vs. pure economic loss) is completely unforeseeable, the defendant may not be liable.

#### **C. The "Eggshell Skull" Rule (Physical Injury)**
*   **Case Context:** The text mentions that the defendant is responsible for the full extent of physical damage, even if the victim's pre-existing condition makes the damage much worse than expected.

#### **D. Proximate Cause and Remote Risks**
*   **Case Context (Palsgraf/Wagner logic):** The text implies that if the risk is too remote or the connection is too attenuated (unlikely), the defendant is not liable.
*   **The "Eggshell Skull" Doctrine (Revisited):** If an action creates a foreseeable risk of *some* harm, the defendant is liable for the full extent of the harm, even if the severity is unexpected.
