### 🧪 Part 1: Sampling Theory and Best Practices

**Key Insight:** Sampling is necessary because big data sets are often too large, or the target population is too difficult, expensive, or impossible to study entirely. The goal of sampling is to minimize bias so that results can be generalized to the larger population.

#### **Core Concepts & Challenges:**
*   **Generalizability:** This is the extent to which results from a sample can be confidently applied to the entire population.
    *   **Critical Point:** If the sample is *not* drawn randomly, generalizability is severely limited.
*   **Sampling Error:** This is the inevitable difference between a sample statistic (e.g., sample mean) and the true value of the entire population.
*   **Qualitative vs. Quantitative Research:**
    *   **Qualitative (Small-n):** Focuses on deep analysis of a small number of cases (can be as few as 1, called a single case study). Statistical tests are not the primary focus.
    *   **Quantitative (Large-n):** Focuses on large numbers of cases and relies on statistical tests.

#### **Sample Size Calculation:**
For quantitative research, a larger sample is generally better. Calculating the necessary size requires three key pieces of information:
1.  **Size of the Population ($N$):** The total number of possible cases.
2.  **Margin of Error:** How far off the result can be from the true population value (lower margin requires a larger sample).
3.  **Confidence Level:** How confident the researcher wants to be (usually 95%).

#### **Sampling Methods (How to Select Units):**
Sampling techniques are divided into two broad categories:

**1. Probability Samples (Best for Generalizability):**
*   **Definition:** Every unit in the population has a *known* and potentially *equal* chance of being included.
*   **Simple Random Sample:** All units are in one group, and selection is done purely by chance (e.g., using a random number generator).
*   **Stratified Sample:** The population is divided into relevant subgroups (strata), and then random samples are drawn from *each* stratum.
*   **Cluster Sample:** The population is divided into clusters (groups), and then entire *clusters* are randomly selected and studied.

**2. Non-Probability Samples (Used when randomization is impossible or undesirable):**
*   **Definition:** The chance of inclusion is not known or is not equal for all units. Generalizability is significantly weaker.
*   **Convenience Sampling:** Selecting participants who are easiest to reach (e.g., stopping people on the street). *Weakest method for generalizability.*
*   **Quota Sampling:** Setting specific quotas or categories of interest and accepting cases until those categories are filled (still considered a convenience sample).
*   **Purposive Sampling:** The researcher deliberately chooses participants who possess specific characteristics relevant to the study (common in qualitative research).
*   **Snowball Sampling:** Used when the target group is hard to find. Initial subjects are interviewed and then asked to recommend or introduce the researcher to other relevant people.

### 🔬 Part 2: The Classic Experiment

**Key Insight:** An experiment is a powerful research method because the researcher actively **manipulates** variables in a controlled setting. This process maximizes control, leading to more reliable and valid findings compared to observational studies.

#### **Features of a True Experiment:**
1.  **Control:** Preventing outside factors from interfering. This is achieved by maintaining systematic procedures and using a **Control Group** (the group that does not receive the treatment) alongside the **Treatment Group** (the group that is manipulated).
2.  **Manipulation:** The researcher must have the ability to actively change (administer a treatment to) the independent variable.
3.  **Random Selection of Participants:** Using a random sample increases **External Validity**—the likelihood that the results apply to the wider population.
4.  **Random Assignment of Subjects to Groups:** Participants are randomly placed into either the control or treatment group, which minimizes **Selection Bias**.

#### **Validity: The Measures of Quality:**
*   **Internal Validity:** The extent to which the researcher can be reasonably sure that the observed results were caused *only* by the independent variable (within the study).
*   **External Validity:** The extent to which the results from the sample can be generalized to the wider, real-world population.

#### **Threats to Validity (Problems to Watch Out For):**
*   **Threats to Internal Validity:** (Problems that undermine the certainty of cause/effect within the study.)
    *   **Reactivity:** Participants change their natural behavior simply because they know they are being observed.
    *   **Testing Effects:** Subjects improve their scores on a measure simply because they took the test multiple times, regardless of treatment.
    *   **History Effects:** Unforeseen outside events that occur during the study and affect the subjects.
    *   **Maturation:** Subjects naturally change (in beliefs, attitudes, etc.) over the length of the study, independent of the treatment.
    *   **Mortality:** When subjects drop out of the study; if those who drop out are systematic (e.g., only the sickest people), the results are flawed.
    *   **Diffusion Effects:** When the treatment intended only for the treatment group leaks or spreads to the control group.

*   **Threats to External Validity:** (Problems that undermine the applicability of the results outside the study.)
    *   (Often caused by the high degree of control in the experiment, making it too artificial to reflect the real world.)
