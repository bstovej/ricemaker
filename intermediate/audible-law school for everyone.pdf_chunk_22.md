This document segment provides a legal analysis of **Strict Liability** as applied to three distinct areas: animals, ultrahazardous activities (blasting), and products liability.

Below are the key insights and facts organized by topic.

### 1. Liability for Animals
The law distinguishes between domestic animals and wild animals to determine when a owner is liable for injuries.

*   **The Evolution of the "One-Bite Rule":** 
    *   **Traditional Approach:** Under common law, the "one-bite rule" applied. Owners were held to a **negligence** standard (liable only if they knew the animal was dangerous) until the animal bit someone, at which point the standard shifted to **strict liability**.
    /
    *   **Modern Approach:** Most modern courts have abolished the one-bite rule. Now, any sign of "abnormally dangerous propensity" (e.g., lunging or baring teeth) is sufficient to trigger strict liability.
*   **Wild Animals:** Owners of wild animals (e.g., tigers, cobras) are subject to **strict liability** regardless of the precautions taken to prevent escape.
    *   **Defining "Wild":** A distinction is made based on two factors: a lack of domestication and a high likelihood of causing injury. This creates legal ambiguity for "exotic" animals that may not be inherently high-risk (e.g., the "zebra" problem).
*   **The Zoo Exception:** There is a controversial legal exception for zoos. Because zoos provide public utility (education/entertainment), some courts apply a **negligence** standard rather than strict liability to avoid disincentivizing zoo operations.

### 2. Ultrahazardous Activities (Blasting)
Certain activities are deemed so inherently dangerous that they trigger strict liability.

*   **Scope of Application:** Activities such as blasting, explosions, fumigation, gasoline storage, and plutonium processing are categorized as **ultrahazardous or abnormally dangerous**.
*   **The Contextual Rule:** Whether an activity is considered "ultrahazardous" often depends on **common usage and context**. For example, gasoline storage might be ultrahazardous in a residential neighborhood but not in an industrial refinery.
*   **Fireworks Controversy:** While some older courts exempted fireworks due to their "public benefit" (similar to the zoo exception), modern law often maintains strict liability for them, arguing that public utility does not exempt an activity from the strict liability standard.

The text outlines the historical shift from "privity of contract" to "strict liability" for manufacturers.

*   **The End of "Privity":** Historically, a consumer could only sue the person they directly bought a product from. Modern law has moved away from this, allowing downstream consumers to sue manufacturers.
*   **Key Legal Precedents:**
    *   **MacPherson v. Buick (implied):** The transition from needing a direct contract to allowing liability for foreseeable harm.
    *   **The Role of Cardozo/MacPherson (Contextual):** The movement toward holding manufacturers liable for defects that cause foreseeable injury.
*   **The Transition to Strict Liability:** The shift from proving a manufacturer was *negligent* to simply proving the product was *defective* and caused harm.
