### ⚖️ I. Research Ethics and Institutional Review (IRB)

The document places a strong emphasis on ethical compliance, noting that ethical breaches carry severe professional and institutional consequences.

**Key Facts:**

*   **Core Ethical Requirement:** Researchers must demonstrate that they are aware of potential risks (including psychological, emotional, and reputational harm) and are capable of protecting subjects from harm.
*   **IRB Review Categories (Three Levels):**
    1.  **Exempt:** Research that imposes no foreseeable risk to subjects.
    2.  **Expedited:** Research posing *minimal risk* (harm is no greater than what might happen in daily life).
    3.  **Full Board Review:** Covers everything else, including intentional deception, invasive procedures, sensitive/stressful topics, or potential for more than minimal harm.
*   **IRB Evaluation Standard (Regardless of Level):** The IRB must ensure three things:
    1.  Risks to subjects have been minimized.
    2.  Researchers are ready and able to care for subjects and data.
    3.  Any potential harm is *outweighed by the potential benefits* of the research.
*   **Consequences of Violation:** Violations can lead to retraction of published work, loss of federal funding, disciplinary action against the individual, and potential loss of standing for the research institution.

### 🧭 II. Developing the Research Question (Lec 4)

A critical shift is highlighted: research must move from a general **Topic** (area of interest) to a focused **Question** (answerable problem).

**Key Insights on Good Questions:**

Good research questions generally share three main characteristics:

1.  **Unanswered:** The question must aim to produce new, original knowledge. This does not mean the topic is completely unknown; rather, it means the question needs to fill a *gap* in existing scholarly debate or challenge established assumptions.
2.  **Appropriate in Scope:** The question must be manageable given the resources, time, available data, and the anticipated final product (e.g., a single paper vs. a program evaluation).
3.  **Empirical:** The question must ask "how the world works" and be based on observable events, phenomena, and actors.
    *   **Contrast: Empirical vs. Normative:**
        *   **Empirical Questions:** Objective, observational (e.g., *What* are the current effects of policy X?).
        *   **Normative Questions:** Subjective judgments about how the world *should* work (e.g., *What policy ought to be* implemented?).
    *   *Guidance:* Normative questions often *motivate* the work, but to answer them scientifically, one must use **empirical** research.

**Practical Tips for Formulation:**

*   **Strategy:** Start with the 5 Ws and H (Who, What, Where, When, Why, How).
*   **Avoid:** Yes/No questions (these lead to trivial answers) and assumptions about the answer (e.g., instead of "Is X better than Y?", ask "What is the most effective way to achieve Z?").
*   **Best Practice:** It is better to answer a narrow question well than to attempt a large question poorly.

### 📚 III. The Literature Review (Lec 5)

The literature review is foundational; it is the scholarly background work that proves the research is necessary and valuable.

**Key Functions and Facts:**

*   **Definition:** The literature review encompasses both the *process* of finding and evaluating existing body of scholarly work, and the *section* of the report that summarizes these findings.
*   **Purpose:** To demonstrate that the research is adding genuine value by:
    *   Identifying gaps in current knowledge.
    *   Mapping out scholarly debates and conflicting theories.
    *   Learning established best practices, common definitions, and potential mistakes from previous studies.
*   **Scholarly Source Criteria (Critical):** For a source to be considered scholarly, it must meet **all three** criteria:
    1.  **Written by Experts:** Author must hold a terminal degree or be affiliated with a reputable research institution.
    2.  **Written for Other Scholars:** The intended audience must be academics/researchers, not the general public.
    3.  **Undergone Peer Review:** Other experts must have evaluated the work for merit (best found in university press books or academic journals).
*   **Nonscholarly Source Warning:** Sources like newspaper articles, blogs, or magazines, even if written by experts, are typically *not* considered scholarly for literature reviews unless they are used for contextual background only.

**Source Finding Strategies:**

*   **Snowball Method:** Find 1-2 highly relevant, recent scholarly articles, and then systematically track their bibliographies and the bibliographies of the articles cited within those.
*   **Citation Count:** Use tools (like Google Scholar) to find articles with a high citation count, as this indicates they are core parts of the literature.
*   **Databases:** Use comprehensive academic databases (JSTOR, EBSCO, etc.) and refine results using provided subject headings.
