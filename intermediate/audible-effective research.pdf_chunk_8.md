## 📊 I. Principles of Survey Design and Question Writing

**Goal:** To gather necessary information on independent, dependent, and control variables while maintaining objectivity.

### Key Insights:
*   **Incentives:** Offering minor incentives (like gift cards) can significantly increase participation rates.
*   **Demographics:** These questions (sex, age, income, race, etc.) are vital for comparing subgroups within the respondents but should be placed near the end of the survey.
*   **Caution:** Demographic questions must be carefully worded, or they may be perceived as irrelevant or raise sensitive issues, causing respondents to quit.

### Rules for Writing Effective Questions:
1.  **Language:** Must be **plain, neutral, and objective**. Avoid technical jargon; if jargon must be used, provide a clear definition.
2.  **Bias:** Never use judgmental words or try to influence the respondent's answer.
3.  **Absolutes:** Avoid words like "always," "never," and "every" to prevent untruthful responses or lack of useful data.
4.  **Double-Barreled Questions:** Never ask two distinct questions in one. This can lead to results that are impossible to interpret accurately.

### Response Options (Close-ended vs. Open-ended):
*   **Open-ended:** Allows respondents to write their own answers (unrestricted). *Pro:* Captures novel ideas. *Con:* Difficult to code.
*   **Close-ended (Multiple Choice):** Provides a set of options. *Pro:* Easy to code, fast for respondents. *Con:* Restricts possible answers.
*   **Validity Checks:**
    *   **Exhaustive:** All possible responses must be covered.
    *   **Exclusive:** Answer choices must not overlap (e.g., a person shouldn't fit two categories).

## 🗳️ II. Understanding Scientific Polling

**Definition:** The core distinction is between **Scientific Polls** and **Unscientific Polls**.
*   **Scientific Poll:** Uses a **random sample** (every member of the population has an equal chance of inclusion), allowing results to be generalized to the wider population.
*   **Unscientific Poll:** Uses non-random methods (e.g., calling only landlines, voluntary responses) and cannot reliably generalize findings. (Examples: Straw polls, Literary Digest).

### The 5 Rules of Good Polling:
1.  **Use a Random Sample:** This is the most important element; it allows the use of probability theory to estimate the likelihood that the sample represents the population.
2.  **Use a Large-Enough Sample:** A sample must be large enough to allow random chance to function. For large national populations, a minimum of **1000 people** is generally recommended.
3.  **Minimize Sample Bias:** This involves ensuring the sample composition accurately reflects the population. Be wary of **nonresponse bias** (systematic refusal to participate, e.g., excluding mobile phone users who are younger than landline users).
4.  **Minimize Bias Beyond the Sample:**
    *   **Question/Wording Bias:** How the question is phrased can influence the outcome.
    *   **Social Desirability Bias:** When respondents provide answers they believe are socially acceptable or what the interviewer wants to hear, rather than their true beliefs.
    *   **Sponsor Bias:** Scrutinize results based on the group that paid for or ran the poll, especially if the results confirm their viewpoint.
5.  **Ensure Transparent Methodology:** A reliable poll must report the full process, including:
    *   The exact questions and response options.
    *   All breakdowns (including "undecided" and "unsure").
    *   The **sample size**, **sampling method**, and **response rate**.
    *   **Weighting:** If adjustments are made to reflect demographic realities.
    *   **Date/Context:** When the data was collected.
