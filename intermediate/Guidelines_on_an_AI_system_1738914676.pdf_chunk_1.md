This document contains the **Commission Guidelines on the definition of an artificial intelligence system** under the EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689). The document serves to clarify the scope of the Act by defining exactly what constitutes an "AI system."

Below are the key insights and facts categorized by theme:

### 1. Core Legal Context
*   **Effective Date:** The definition of an AI system and certain provisions of the AI Act (such as prohibited practices) entered into application on **February 2, 2025**.
*   **Purpose of Guidelines:** The guidelines are intended to help providers and stakeholders determine if a technology falls under the regulation's scope. They are **not legally binding**; only the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) provides authoritative interpretations.
*   **Scope Strategy:** The definition is intentionally flexible to accommodate rapid technological changes. It does not require an exhaustive list of all possible AI systems but focuses on specific characteristics.

### 2. The Seven Elements of an AI System
To be classified as an AI system under Article 3(1), a technology must meet seven main criteria:
1.  **Machine-based:** Must run on hardware and software (including quantum or even biological/organic systems, provided they offer computational capacity).
2.  **Varying levels of autonomy:** Must operate with some degree of independence from human intervention (excludes purely manual tools).
3.  **Potential adaptiveness:** The system *may* (but does not have to) change its behavior after deployment via self-learning.
4.  **Explicit or implicit objectives:** Must operate toward specific goals (e.g., optimizing a cost function or achieving a programmed task).
*   **Inference mechanism:** The system must be able to **infer** how to generate outputs from inputs.
*   **Output generation:** The outputs can take the form of predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions.
*   **Environmental influence:** The outputs must be capable of influencing physical or virtual environments.

### 3. Key Technical Distinctions
*   **The "Inference" Threshold:** This is the **indispensable condition** that distinguishes AI from traditional software. Traditional software that simply executes manual, human-defined rules without the ability to "infer" or derive models/algorithms is **outside the scope** of the AI Act.
*   **The Lifecycle Approach:** The definition covers two phases: the **"building" phase** (pre-deployment) and the **"use" phase** (post-deployment). Not all seven elements must be present during both phases.
*   **Autonomy vs. Adaptiveness:** 
    *   **Autonomy** is a necessary condition (the system needs some independence).
    *   **Adaptiveness** (self-learning) is **facultative** (not required); a system can be an AI system even if it does not learn from new data after it is deployed.

### 4. Recognized AI Techniques
The guidelines categorize AI techniques into two primary branches:
*   **Machine Learning (ML) Approaches:**
    *   **Supervised Learning:** Learning from labeled data (e.g., spam filters, image classification).
    *   **Unsupervised Learning:** Finding patterns in unlabeled data (e.g., drug discovery, clustering).
    *   **Self-supervised Learning:** The system creates its own labels (e.g., Large Language Models predicting the next token).
    *   **Reinforcement Learning:** Learning through trial and error via a reward function (e.g., robotics, autonomous vehicles).
    *   **Deep Learning:** A subset of ML using neural networks to learn features from raw data.
*   **Logic- and Knowledge-based Approaches:**
    *   These systems do not learn from data but infer from encoded human knowledge, rules, or symbolic representations (e.g., early medical expert systems).

### 5. Summary of Exclusions
The document explicitly states that the following are **not** AI systems:
*   **Traditional Software:** Systems based solely on rules defined by humans to automatically execute operations.
*   **Limited Inference Systems:** Systems that may have a narrow capacity to infer but lack the ability to analyze patterns or adjust outputs autonomously.
