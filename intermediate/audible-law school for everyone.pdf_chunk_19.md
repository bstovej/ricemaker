This document provides a foundational overview of key concepts in **Tort Law**, specifically focusing on the "Standard of Care" and how the law determines whether a person’s actions were negligent.

Below are the key insights and facts categorized by legal principle.

### 1. The Duty to Rescue and Moral vs. Legal Obligation
*   **The Duty to Rescue:** The text cites a statute requiring individuals to provide "reasonable assistance" to someone in grave physical harm, provided the assistance can be rendered without danger to the rescuer or interference with other important duties.
*   **Legal vs. Moral Tension:** A central theme is the "gap" between moral and legal obligations. While there is a high moral obligation to help others, the legal obligation is much narrower.
*   **The Surgeon Hypothetical:** The text uses the "Lord Macaulay" hypothetical to illustrate how duty might change based on variables like distance (travel across a continent), compensation, and proximity (the hospital down the street).

### 2. The "Reasonable Person" Standard
*   **The Objective Standard:** Tort law uses an **objective standard**, meaning the law looks at the defendant's actions from the outside rather than considering their subjective intent or "trying their best."
*   **The Standard of Conduct:** The "reasonable person" is a legal fiction—a person who acts with the level of care that a prudent person would in similar circumstances.
*   **Predictability:** The goal of this standard is to create a consistent benchmark for behavior, regardless of an individual's specific temperament or mental state.

### 3. Adjustments to the Standard (Disabilities and Expertise)
The law does not apply a "one size fits all" approach; it adjusts the standard based on specific physical or professional contexts:
*   **Physical Limitations:** The standard is adjusted for physical disabilities (e.g., a blind person is judged by the standard of a reasonable person with that specific visual impairment).
*   **Professional Expertise:** If a person possesses specialized knowledge (like a doctor or engineer), they are held to a higher standard of care consistent with that professional expertise.
*   **Children/Age:** The text implies that age and maturity (e.g., "infancy" or "children") are factors in determining the standard of care.

### 4. Rules Regarding "Custom" and "Industry Standard"
The document explores how "custom" (what is commonly done in an industry) interacts with the law:
*   **Industry Custom is Not Absolute:** Just because an entire industry follows a specific practice (custom) does *not* mean that practice is legally safe or "reasonable."
*   **The "Industry Standard" Fallacy:** As demonstrated by the *T.J. Hooper* reference, an entire industry can be found negligent if their common practice is unsafe (e.g., failing to carry lifeboats).
*   **Industry Custom as Evidence:** While custom is not a defense against negligence, it can be used as evidence to help a jury decide what a "reasonable person" would do.

### 5. Legal Mechanisms: Negligence vs. Negligence Per Se
*   **Negligence Per Se:** This occurs when a person violates a specific statute (like a traffic law) and that violation leads to an injury. In these cases, the violation of the law itself can be used to establish that the person breached their duty of care.
*   **Negligence Per Se vs. General Negligence:** While general negligence requires proving a breach of the "reasonable person" standard, "negligence per se" relies on the violation of a codified law.
