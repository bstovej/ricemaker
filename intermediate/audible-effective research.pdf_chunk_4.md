## 💡 I. The Literature Review: Synthesis, Not Summary

**Key Insight:** A literature review is not a source-by-source summary; it is an argument that *synthesizes* existing knowledge to identify what is known, what is unknown, and what needs to be debated.

**Core Facts & Steps:**

1. **Active Note-Taking is Crucial:** When reading, take notes on three specific elements for every source:
    *   Its core **claims**.
    *   Its **research design** (methodology).
    *   Its **implications** (what it suggests for future study).
2. **Maintain Objectivity:** Always clearly distinguish between the authors’ claims and *your own analysis* or critical thinking.
3. **Identify the "Gaps":** The primary purpose of writing the review is to:
    *   **Establish Consensus:** What is generally agreed upon (e.g., definitions, common theories)?
    *   **Highlight Gaps:** What issues or ideas have authors missed that you believe are important?
    *   **Map Debates:** Where do scholars disagree, and how can your project enter that debate?
4. **Organizational Focus:** Structure the review around *themes, gaps, debates, or methodological types* rather than organizing it by source (which would result in an annotated bibliography).

## 🔬 II. Generating Theories and Hypotheses

**Key Insight:** Theory provides the "why" or "how" (explanation), while hypotheses provide a specific, testable prediction. Both are guided by the scientific principle of testability.

### A. Theories
*   **Definition:** Generalized explanations for patterns observed in the world; they help understand causal mechanisms.
*   **Development:** Researchers move from **Observation $\rightarrow$ Theory $\rightarrow$ Test (against new data)**.
*   **Warning:** Be careful to avoid **data fitting** (creating a theory that only explains the data you already possess).
*   **Principle of Parsimony:** Simpler explanations are generally preferred, provided they remain accurate and do not lose essential causal power.

### B. Hypotheses
*   **Definition:** Testable, probabilistic statements that represent the researcher’s best guess at an answer to the research question.
*   **The Null Hypothesis ($\text{H}_0$):** This is the standard hypothesis tested in research. It simply states that **no relationship exists** between the variables. The goal is to disprove the null hypothesis.
*   **Scientific Standard (Popper):** Strong hypotheses must be **falsifiable**—meaning they must be written in a way that it is logically possible to disprove them.
*   **Variables:**
    *   **Independent Variable (X):** The variable that is manipulated or presumed to cause change.
    *   **Dependent Variable (Y):** The outcome or effect being measured (what is changing).
*   **Relationship Types:**
    *   **Correlation:** A statement that two variables are related (e.g., as one goes up, the other goes up).
    *   **Causation:** A statement that one variable actively *causes* a change in another.

## 📊 III. Selecting a Research Design

**Key Insight:** The choice of design dictates the nature of the data collected and the type of questions that can be answered. Designs fall broadly into Quantitative and Qualitative approaches.

### A. Quantitative Approach
*   **Goal:** To test relationships and measure variables using **large amounts of numerical data** via statistical tools.
*   **Methods:**
    *   **Experimental Designs:** The "gold standard" for establishing causality. Requires proper controls, random assignment, and manipulation of variables (high control).
    *   **Cross-sectional Studies:** Measures data on a sample of cases **at a single point in time**.
    *   **Longitudinal Studies:** Measures data on the same cases/group **over an extended period of time**, ideal for observing trends and changes.
    *   **Correlational Studies:** Focuses specifically on identifying statistical relationships between variables, often using existing data.

### B. Qualitative Approach
*   **Goal:** To understand deep meaning and context by studying a **smaller number of cases** in depth, utilizing data that cannot always be reduced to numbers (e.g., interviews, narratives).
