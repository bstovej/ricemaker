### **1. The Craft of Dialogue**
The text outlines specific "dos and don’ts" for writing effective dialogue in nonfiction:
*   **Prioritize Authenticity:** Avoid heavy-handed use of dialect or phonetic spelling to indicate accents; this can become distracting. Instead, use context to convey character.
*   **Avoid "Expository" Dialogue:** Do not use characters to dump information on the reader. Dialogue should feel natural, not like a vehicle for explaining the plot or setting.

*   **Minimize Dialogue Tags:** Excessive use of "he said/she said" or descriptive tags (e.g., "he shouted angrily") can clutter the prose. The goal is to let the conversation and character action speak for themselves.
*   **Use "Less is More":** The most effective dialogue is often sparse. Avoid "over-writing" the way people talk; focus on the essential movement of the conversation.

### **2. The "Nonfiction Contract" and Ethics**
A central theme is the ethical relationship between the author and the reader, often referred to as the "nonfiction contract."
*   **The Duty of Accuracy:** While memory is fallible, the author has an obligation to be as accurate as possible. 
*   **Transparency regarding Memory:** If an author is relying on reconstructed memories (e.g., events from childhood), they should be transparent about this.
*   **Handling Oral History:** When using interviews or letters, the author must maintain the integrity of the source without distorting the original meaning to fit a narrative arc.
*   **Use of Ellipses:** When quoting, an author can use ellipses to remove irrelevant parts of a quote, but they must not use them to change the fundamental meaning or intent of the speaker.

### **3. Research Methodology**
The text provides a framework for conducting deep, investigative research for narrative nonfiction:
*   **Primary vs. Secondary Sources:**
    *   **Primary Sources:** Original materials (letters, diaries, photographs, interviews, official records, artifacts). These are the "raw" materials of history.
    
    *   **Secondary Sources:** Interpretations of primary sources (textbooks, biographies, articles). These provide context but are one step removed from the event.
*   **The "Paper Trail" Strategy:** Effective research involves following a chain of evidence. One document (a letter) might lead to a name, which leads to a person, which leads to a census record, which leads to a location.
*   **Source Triangulation:** A strong narrative is built by corroborating a single fact across multiple, independent sources to ensure accuracy.

### **4. Evaluating Source Credibility**
The text emphasizes the importance of "lateral reading" and critical thinking when vetting information:
*   **Assessing Authority:** Evaluate the author’s credentials and their institutional affiliations (e.g., a university professor vs. an anonymous blogger).
*   **Identifying Bias:** Every source has a perspective. The goal is not necessarily to find a "neutral" source, but to identify the bias (political, social, or personal) so the reader can understand the context of the information.
*   **Checking Provenance:** Investigate where a piece of information came from and how it has changed as it was passed from person to person or document to document.
*   **Domain and Context:** Use digital literacy skills to evaluate the reliability of web domains (e.g., distinguishing between `.gov`/`.edu` and `.com` or personal blogs).
