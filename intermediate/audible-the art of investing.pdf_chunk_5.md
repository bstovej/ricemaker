### 1. Investment Philosophies

**John Templeton: The Global Growth Specialist**
*   **Hybrid Approach:** Templeton did not follow a strict "value" or "growth" box. While he searched for bargains (value), his primary focus (as seen in the Templeton Growth Fund) was on a company's future potential (growth).
*   **Early Socially Responsible Investing (SRI):** He was a pioneer of ESG-style investing, actively avoiding "sin stocks" such as those in the alcohol, tobacco, and gambling industries.
*   **Dividend Strategy:** Unlike traditional value investors who sought high yields, Templeton preferred **dividend growth** over high absolute yields. He believed companies paying no dividends often had more significant upside potential.
*   **Tax Efficiency:** He prioritized maximizing "total return after taxes." He achieved this through low portfolio turnover (holding stocks for roughly 4 years), which minimized the tax drag caused by frequent trading.

**David Dreman: The Contrarian**
*   **Contrarianism:** Dreman’s strategy is built on going against the crowd. He believes that when an industry or stock is out of favor (e.g., the tobacco industry after major lawsuits), it can present a buying opportunity if the liabilities are now finite and manageable.
*   **"In-Depth Profits" over "Information Overload":** Rather than trying to digest all available market data, Dreman advocates for deep, detailed knowledge of a smaller number of companies, focusing on what drives sales, earnings, and margins.
*   **Selection Criteria:** He looks for "quality" companies with high dividends and low valuation multiples (P/E, P/CF, or P/B) that are at least 30% lower than the overall market.
*   **Portfolio Structure:** He prefers a concentrated portfolio of 20–30 stocks diversified across at least 15 industries.

### 3. Behavioral Finance (Psychological Biases)
The document highlights several cognitive biases that lead to poor investment decisions:
*   **Home Bias:** The tendency to over-invest in one's domestic market.
*   **The Disposition Effect/Behavioral Biases:**
    *   **The Disposition Effect:** Selling winners too early and holding losers too long.
    *   **Loss Aversion:** The psychological pain of a loss is greater than the joy of a gain.
    *   **Overreaction/Underreaction:** Investors often overreact to bad news or underreact to long-term positive trends.
*   **Specific Biases Identified:**
    *   **Optimism/Pessimism Bias:** Miscalculating the likelihood of future events.
    *   **Overconfidence Bias:** Overestimating one's ability to predict market movements.
    *   **Recency Bias:** Giving undue weight to recent events (e.g., assuming a current bull market will last forever).
    *   **Confirmation Bias:** Seeking information that supports one's existing beliefs.

### 4. Key Economic & Financial Concepts
*   **Currency Risk:** The danger that fluctuations in exchange rates will erode investment returns.
*   **Dividend/Yield:** The importance of companies paying out a portion of earnings to shareholders.
*   **Valuation Metrics:** The use of P/E (Price-to-Earnings) and other ratios to determine if a stock is overvalued or undervalued.
*   **Market Efficiency:** The debate over whether markets reflect all available information (a central theme in the discussion of "behavioral finance").
