This document provides an overview of the **NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0**, published on February 26, 2024. It serves as a guide for organizations to manage and reduce cybersecurity risks through a flexible, non-prescriptive taxonomy of outcomes.

Below are the key insights and facts categorized by their functional role within the framework.

### 1. Core Purpose and Philosophy
*   **Outcome-Oriented, Not Prescriptive:** The CSF 2.0 does not dictate *how* to achieve security; instead, it defines *what* desirable cybersecurity outcomes look like. It provides the "what" and links to external resources for the "how."
*   **Universal Applicability:** The framework is designed to be "sector-neutral," "country-neutral," and "technology-neutral." It is applicable to any organization regardless of size, maturity, or industry (including industry, government, academia, and non-profits).
*   **Comprehensive Scope:** It covers all types of Information and Communication Technology (ICT), including **IT, IoT (Internet of Things), and OT (Operational Technology)**, as well as modern environments like **Cloud, Mobile, and AI**.
*   **Enterprise Risk Integration:** Ideally, the CSF should be used to manage cybersecurity risks alongside other enterprise risks, such as financial, supply chain, privacy, and reputational risks.

### 2. The Six Functions of the CSF Core
The "Core" is the nucleus of the framework, organized into a hierarchy of **Functions $\rightarrow$ Categories $\rightarrow$ Subcategories**. Version 2.0 organizes these into six functions:

*   **GOVERN (GV) [New/Highlighted]:** The central function. It establishes the strategy, policy, and oversight. It focuses on incorporating cybersecurity into broader Enterprise Risk Management (ERM) and emphasizes **cybersecurity supply chain risk management**.
*   **IDENTIFY (ID):** Understanding the organization’s assets (data, hardware, software, etc.) and the associated risks to prioritize efforts.
*   **PROTECT (PR):** Implementing safeguards (e.g., access control, data security, and training) to prevent or reduce the impact of cyber events.
*   **DETECT (DE):** Developing the capability to find and analyze anomalies or indicators of a compromise in a timely manner.
*   **RESPOND (RS):** Taking action once an incident is detected to contain the effects (e.g., incident management and mitigation).
*   **RECOVER (RC):** Restoring operations and assets affected by an incident to minimize disruption.

### 3. Key Implementation Tools
The framework provides specific mechanisms to help organizations move from their current state to a desired state:

*   **Organizational Profiles:**
    *   **Current Profile:** Documents the cybersecurity outcomes an organization is currently achieving.
    *   **Target Profile:** Defines the desired outcomes the organization aims to achieve based on its specific risk appetite and mission.
    *   **Community Profiles:** Baselines created for specific sectors or use cases that organizations can use as a starting point.
*   **CSF Tiers:** Used to characterize the rigor and complexity of an organization's risk management and governance practices.
*   **The "CSF Portfolio" (Supplementary Resources):** NIST provides an expanding suite of online resources to assist implementation, including:
    *   **Quick Start Guides (QSGs):** Actionable guidance for smaller or new users.
    *   **Informative References:** Links to global standards and regulations.
    *   **Implementation Examples:** Real-world illustrations of how to achieve specific outcomes.

### 4. Strategic Utility
The document identifies three primary ways organizations use the CSF:
1.  **Understand and Assess:** Identify gaps between the current and target states.
2.  **Prioritize:** Align cybersecurity actions with the organization's mission, legal requirements, and resource availability.
3.  **Communicate:** Provide a "common language" to facilitate communication about risk between technical practitioners and non-technical stakeholders (executives, boards, and regulators).
