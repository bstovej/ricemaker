### **Lecture 11: Poetic Diction and Syntax**
This section focuses on how word choice and sentence structure impact the reader's engagement.

*   **Literary Devices:**
    *   **Metaphor:** A direct comparison between two unlike things (e.g., "her eyes were oceans").
    *   **Simile:** A comparison using "like" or "as" (e.g., "her eyes were like the ocean").
    *   **Synecdoche:** Using a part to represent a whole (e.g., using "hands" to mean "people").
    *   **Metonymy:** Using an associated word to represent an idea (e.g., "The White House" to represent the President).
*   **Connotation vs. Denotation:** The lecture emphasizes that words have "connotations" (emotional associations) that go beyond their literal dictionary definitions. For example, "scheme" and "plan" may be synonyms, but "scheme" carries a negative, deceptive connotation.
*   **The Power of Syntax:** Syntax refers to the grammatical ordering of words. 
    *   Consistent use of simple subject-verb-object structures can become boring.
    	*   Varying sentence structure keeps writing engaging.
    	*   Poetic writers (like E.E. Cummings) may manipulate syntax to create specific effects.

### **Lecture 12: The Art of Performance and Delivery**
This section focuses on the importance of how information is presented to an audience.

*   **The Importance of Delivery:** For oral presentations, the effectiveness of the message relies heavily on inflection, emphasis, and tone.
*   **Audience Engagement:** Good speakers must consider how they deliver a message to maintain interest and authority.
*   **The Role of Preparation:** Using Shakespeare’s *Henry V* as a conceptual backdrop, the lecture implies that the "theatricality" of a speech—its rhythm and emotional weight—is vital to its success.
*   **Key takeaway:** A speaker's effectiveness is not just about *what* they say, but *how* they say it (inflection, emphasis, and presence).

### **Lecture 13: Autobiographical Writing and Self-Presentation**
This section explores the nuances of writing about oneself and managing public perception.

*   **The Danger of "TMI":** The lecture warns against providing too much unnecessary information, which can detract from the primary message.
*   **Strategic Self-Presentation:**
    *   **Selectivity:** Effective autobiographical writing requires being selective about which details to include.
    *   **The "IAA" Principle (Implicit):** The text suggests a need to balance interests, abilities, and achievements without appearing boastful.
*   **The Benjamin Franklin Model:** Using Franklin's autobiography as an example, the lecture notes that one should present achievements and "interests" in a way that demonstrates character and growth without appearing arrogant.
*   **Key takeaway:** Successful self-presentation involves a careful balance of sharing enough to be authentic while maintaining enough restraint to remain professional and engaging.
