### 1. Vicarious Liability (*Respondeat Superior*)
This doctrine holds an employer strictly liable for the torts committed by an employee, provided the acts occur within the **"scope of employment."**

*   **Key Facts:**
    *   **No Fault Required:** The employer does not need to be personally negligent; the mere fact that the employee committed a tort within their scope triggers liability.
    *   **Universality:** This is a near-universal principle in common law (US, UK, Canada, Australia) and civil law (France, Germany) systems.
*   **Rationales for Liability:**
    *   **Evidentiary:** It is easier for victims to identify and sue the corporate entity (the "source" of the harm) than a specific, potentially transient employee.
    *   **Cost Internalization:** Companies should absorb the costs of their operations to create incentives for better quality control and safety.
    *   **Loss Spreading:** Companies are better positioned to distribute the cost of "freakish" losses through insurance or increased product pricing.
*   **The "Scope of Employment" Debate:**
    *   The primary legal conflict is determining where an employee's actions end and their personal "frolic" begins.
    *   **Three Interpretations of Scope:**
        1.  **Restrictive:** Liability only if the employer expressly authorized or encouraged the act.
/
        2.  **Interest-based:** Liability exists if the employee was acting to further the employer's interests.
        3.  **Foreseeability-based:** Liability exists if the employee's misconduct was a foreseeable consequence of doing business.
    *   **Terminology:** A **"detour"** (a minor deviation) usually stays within the scope of employment, whereas a **"frolic"** (a major departure for personal reasons) does not.

### 2. Joint and Several Liability
This applies when multiple defendants (joint tortfeasors) contribute to a single injury.

*   **Key Facts:**
    *   **The "Deep Pockets" Rule:** Under traditional joint and several liability, a plaintiff can recover the **entire amount** of the claim from any single defendant, regardless of that defendant's individual share of the fault.
    *   **The Insolvency Problem:** If one defendant is "judgment proof" (bankrupt), the remaining solvent defendants must cover the entire debt, even if they were only partially at fault.
*   **Legal Debate:** Critics argue this is unfair to wealthy defendants; proponents argue it ensures the injured party is compensated. Modern trends include "plain liability" or apportioning fault to avoid placing the burden on a single defendant.

### 3. Liability Regarding Plaintiffs (The Defenses)
The text outlines how a defendant's liability can be reduced or eliminated based on the plaintiff's actions:

*   **Contributory/Comparative Negligence:**
    *   **Contributory Negligence:** An older, harsher standard where if the plaintiff is even slightly at fault, they are barred from any recovery.
*   **Assumption of Risk (Contributory Negligence):**
    *   When a plaintiff voluntarily engages in a known danger (e.g., extreme sports), they may be barred from recovery.
*   **Avoidable Consequences:**
    *   A plaintiff cannot recover for damages that could have been avoided through reasonable care (e.g., an injury from a car accident is compensable, but the subsequent infection from failing to seek medical help may not be).

### 4. Strict Liability and Animals
*   **Strict Liability:** Liability imposed without a finding of fault or negligence.
*   **Animal Liability:** The text notes a distinction in how the law treats animals. Generally, the owner of a domestic animal (like a dog) is held to a negligence standard (did they act reasonably?), whereas the owner of "dangerous" animals or certain wild animals may be subject to strict liability.
