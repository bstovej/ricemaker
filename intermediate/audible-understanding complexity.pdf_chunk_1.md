### **Core Definitions of Complexity**
*   **The Definition of a Complex System:** A system is considered complex if the **"whole transcends the parts."** 
*   **The Four Pillars of Complexity:** For a system to be classified as complex, its agents must meet four specific qualifications:
    1.  **Diversity:** A variety of different entities.
    2.  **Connection:** The entities are linked.
    3.  **Interdependence:** The actions of one part affect others.
    4.  **Adaptation:** The entities respond to their local and global environments.
*   **Complexity vs. Complicated:** The document makes a sharp distinction between the two. A **complicated** system may have diverse parts, but it lacks **adaptability**. A complex system is adaptive, robust, and capable of producing large-scale, unpredictable events.
*   **Emergence:** This is the phenomenon where macro-level properties arise from micro-level interactions and bear no resemblance to the individual parts (e.g., the "wetness" of water or a heartbeat).

### **The Landscape Model (Problem-Solving Framework)**
The course uses "landscapes" as a mathematical and metaphorical way to understand how we navigate problems and optimization:
*   **Simple Landscapes (Mount Fuji):** Single-peaked. There is only one "best" solution (the global peak), making it easy to optimize (e.g., early industrial "Taylorism").
*   **Rugged Landscapes:** Multiple peaks and valleys. Finding the highest point (the global peak) is difficult because of the "combinatorial explosion" of possibilities. This is caused by **interactions within an agent's own choices.**
*   **Dancing Landscapes:** The peaks and valleys change over time. This is the true hallmark of complexity. It is caused by **interdependencies between the actions of different actors** who are all adapting simultaneously.

### **System Behaviors and Classifications**
The document references physicist Stephen Wolfram’s four classes of system behaviors to describe how systems function:
*   **Class 1 (Stable):** Single-point equilibria; systems that are resistant to change (e.g., a ball at the bottom of a bowl).
*   **Class 2 (Periodic):** Regular, repeating sequences (e.g., a traffic light cycle).
*   **Class 3 (Chaotic):** Highly sensitive and unpredictable.
*   **Class 4 (Complex/Interesting):** Systems that possess structure and can hold information (the focus of the course).

### **Key Theoretical Insights**
*   **The "Interesting" Middle Ground:** The author suggests that "interesting" complexity is found in the "in-between"—not in total randomness (chaos) and not in total predictability (stasis), but in the transition zones.
*   **Complexity as a Function of Interdependence:** Complexity arises when agents do not act in isolation but are part of a web of interdependence where their actions affect others, and vice versa.
*   **The Role of Encoding:** How we "encode" or perceive a problem can change the landscape from simple to complex.
