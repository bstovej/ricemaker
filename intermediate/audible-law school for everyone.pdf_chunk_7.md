### 1. The Shift in Legal Interpretation: From Judges to Textualism
The document describes a fundamental shift in how laws are interpreted, moving from judicial discretion toward **textualism** (prioritizing the literal text of a statute).

*   **The Case of *Brogan v. United States* (1998):** This case serves as the primary example of the decline of judicial "exceptions." James Brogan was convicted of making a false statement after simply saying "no" to federal agents. 
*   **The Death of the "Exemption" Doctrine:** Previously, judges might have used discretion to prevent unfair outcomes (like punishing a simple "no"). However, the Supreme Court's ruling emphasized that if the law is written broadly, judges cannot create exceptions based on fairness.
*   **Consequences of Textualism:**
    *   **Increased Power for Legislatures:** Because judges now follow the literal text, the wording used by lawmakers is more consequential than ever.
    *   **Expansion of Prosecutorial Power:** The shift favors the state, as it limits the ability of defendants to argue for "fairness" when the law is technically met.
*   **Rise of "Legalism":** The focus has shifted from the *intent* behind a law to the *plain meaning* of its words.

### 2. The Mechanics of Intent: *Mens Rea* and Liability
The text outlines how criminal responsibility is determined through the lens of intent and the "guilty mind."

*   **The Spectrum of Intent:**
    *   **General Intent:** The intent to perform the act itself (e.s., the intent to swing a bat).
    *   **Specific Intent:** The intent to achieve a specific result (e.g., the intent to strike someone with the bat).
*   **Mistake of Fact/Law:**
    *   **Mistake of Fact:** A mistake regarding the situation (e.g., taking someone else's umbrella thinking it is yours) may negate criminal intent.
    *   **Mistake of Law:** Generally, "ignorance of the law is no excuse," but the text notes that in some cases, a mistake of fact can negate the "specific intent" required for a crime.
*   **Strict Liability:** In certain regulatory or "public welfare" offenses, the prosecution does not need to prove intent; the mere commission of the act is enough for a conviction (e.g., certain food/drug regulations).

### 3. Homicide and the Hierarchy of Culpability
The document outlines how the law categorizes the "wrongness" of taking a life based on the level of intent/malice.

*   **Causation:** To be held liable for homicide, the defendant's actions must be the "proximate cause" of death—meaning the death was a foreseeable consequence of the act.
*   **Degrees of Homicide:**
    *   **First-Degree Murder:** Characterized by "premeditation and deliberation" (planned, cold-blooded killing).
    *   **Second-Degree Murder:** Characterized by "malice aforethought" but without a specific plan (e.g., an intentional killing that was impulsive).
    *   **Manslaughter:**
        *   **Voluntary Manslaughter:** Killing in the "heat of passion" (e.g., finding a spouse in an affair), which may mitigate the charge from murder.
        *   **Involuntary Manslaughter:** Death resulting from extreme negligence or recklessness (e.g., a car accident caused by drunk driving).
