This document segment covers three lectures regarding the evolution of American criminal law, focusing on federal power, the Eighth Amendment, and the Due Process Clause.

### **Lecture 5: Federal Crimes and Federal Power**

**Key Facts:**
* **Expansion of Federal Authority:** Historically, federal crimes were few. The scope of federal criminal law grew as the federal government’s power to regulate **interstate and foreign commerce** expanded.
* **The Commerce Clause "Apogee":** In ***Wickard v. Filburn* (1942)**, the Supreme Court reached the peak of this expansion, ruling that Congress can regulate purely local (intrastable) activity if it has an effect on interstate commerce.
* **The Limit of Federal Power:** In ***United States v. Lopez* (1995)**, the Court established a limit, ruling that the Gun Free School Zones Act exceeded Congress's power because the connection to interstate commerce was too "speculative and remote."
* **The Marijuana Conflict:** A significant legal tension exists because many states have legalized marijuana while it remains a prohibited substance under federal law. This creates practical issues in banking and the ability of lawyers to provide legal advice without risking disbarment.

**Key Insights:**
* **Complexity and Intent:** In complex federal regulatory crimes, corporate defendants are rarely excused by "ignorance of the law." However, for "average citizens," the Court provides two protections: the requirement that a defendant must know the **facts** that make conduct illegal, and an "honest mistake" defense regarding complex statutes (like the tax code).
* **The Evolution of Commerce:** The expansion of federal law is directly tied to the transformation of the American economy from local/regional to national/global.

### **Lecture 6: Cruel and Unusual Punishments (8th Amendment)**

**Key Facts:**
* ***Harmelin v. Michigan* (1991):** The Court upheld a mandatory life sentence without parole for cocaine possession. 
    * **Justice Scalia’s view:** The 8th Amendment only prohibits "cruel methods" (e.g., the rack) rather than disproportionate sentences.
    * **Justice Kennedy’s view:** The Amendment prohibits both cruel methods and punishments that are disproportionate to the crime.
* ***Roper v. Simmons* (2005):** The Court ruled that the death penalty cannot be applied to defendants who were under 18 at the time of their crime, citing "evolving standards of decency."

**Key Insights:**
* **The "Counter-Majoritarian" Dilemma:** The Court must balance constitutional protections against the "will of the people" (e.g., determining what constitutes "cruel and unusual" punishment).
* **The Concept of Evolving Standards:** The interpretation of the 8th Amendment is not static; it shifts based on societal changes (as seen in *Roper*).
* **The Counter-Majoritarian Difficulty:** The judiciary often makes decisions (like in *Roper*) that may conflict with popular opinion or state laws, acting as a check on the majority.

### **Lecture 7: Due Process and the Bill of Rights**

**Key Insights:**
* **The 14th Amendment as a Bridge:** While the Bill of Rights originally only applied to the federal government, the 14th Amendment's Due Process Clause allows the Supreme Court to apply these protections to the states.
* **Fundamental Fairness:** The "Due Process" standard has evolved from mere procedural adherence to a requirement of "fundamental fairness" in how states treat citizens.

### **Summary of Legal Doctrines Mentioned**
* **Commerce Clause Power:** The constitutional basis for federal regulation of interstate activity.
* **The Doctrine of Incorporation (Implicit):** The process by which the Bill of Rights is applied to state governments via the 14th Amendment.
* **The Principle of Legality:** The idea that criminal laws must be clear and that individuals must have fair notice of what constitutes a crime.
