This document segment provides a detailed legal analysis of **Article 5(1)(d) of the EU AI Act**, which prohibits certain types of AI systems used for predicting criminal activity. It outlines the boundaries of this prohibition, the specific exclusions, and how it interacts with other legal frameworks.

Below are the key insights and facts categorized by theme:

### 1. The Core Prohibition and Its Limits
*   **The Ban:** The AI Act prohibits AI systems that assess or predict the risk of a **natural person** committing a criminal offence based **solely** on profiling or the assessment of their personal characteristics/traits.
*   **The Primary Exclusion:** The prohibition **does not apply** if the AI system is used to **support a human assessment** that is already based on **objective and verifiable facts** directly linked to criminal activity.
*   **Key Distinction:** The AI should act as a support tool for humans, not as the sole decision-maker. The human assessment must be the primary driver, utilizing factual evidence rather than just algorithmic profiling.

### 2. Scope of Application (Who and What is Covered)
*   **Natural Persons vs. Legal Entities:**
    *   The prohibition applies only to **natural persons** (individuals).
    *   AI used to profile **legal entities** (e.g., assessing the risk of a company committing tax fraud) is **outside the scope** of this specific prohibition.
    *   **Exception:** If a "sole trader" or independent professional (e.g., a lawyer) is being profiled, the prohibition **does apply** because they are a natural person.
*   **Criminal vs. Administrative Offences:**
    *   The prohibition applies only to **criminal offences**.
    *   AI used to predict or assess **administrative offences** (e.g., minor traffic violations or procurement irregularities) is **outside the scope**.
*   **Law Enforcement vs. Private Actors:**
    *   **Law Enforcement:** Are the primary subjects of this regulation.
    *   **Private Actors:** Can be subject to the prohibition if they are:
        1.  Entrusted by law to exercise public authority (e.g., crime prevention).
        2.  Explicitly requested by law enforcement to perform risk predictions.
        3.  Subject to legal obligations to assess risk (e.g., banks performing **Anti-Money Laundering (AML)** checks).
    *   **Exemption for Private Business:** Routine business profiling (e.g., a bank detecting financial irregularities for its own protection) is **not prohibited**, provided the criminal prediction is not the primary purpose.

### 3. "Person-Based" vs. "Location-Based" AI
*   **Location-Based (Out of Scope):** AI that predicts **where** crime might happen (geospatial/place-based) is generally **not prohibited** because it does not assess a specific individual (e.g., predicting burglary hotspots or using acoustic sensors to detect gunshots).
*   **The "Borderline" Risk:** If a location-based system links a specific location (like a person's residence) to an individual’s risk score, it shifts from "place-based" to "person-based" and may fall under the **prohibition**.

### 4. Requirements for High-Risk AI Systems
If an AI system falls under the "exclusion" (meaning it is allowed because it supports human assessment), it is classified as a **high-risk AI system**. This triggers strict requirements:
*   **Human Oversight:** Oversight must be performed by competent, trained individuals who can interpret outputs and understand the system's limitations.
*   **Mitigating "Automation Bias":** Humans must be trained to avoid over-relying on the AI's output and must be able to intervene or stop the system if it fails.
*   **Verifiable Evidence:** The human's assessment must ensure that any prediction is rooted in objective and verifiable facts.

### 5. Legal Interplay and Case Law
*   **CJEU Case Law (*Ligue des droits humains*):** The document references this case to emphasize that human assessment in automated processes must rely on objective criteria to prevent discrimination and false positives.
*   **Interplay with Other Laws:** The AI Act does not operate in a vacuum; it must be read alongside:
    *   **GDPR & LED (Law Enforcement Directive):** Regarding the lawfulness of processing personal data and preventing discriminatory profiling.
    *   **Presumption of Innocence:** The AI Act applies even at the "prediction" stage (before a formal investigation), whereas the Presumption of Innocence Directive applies once a person is a suspect or accused.
    *   **Criminal Law Standards:** The determination of what constitutes a "criminal" offense (as opposed to administrative) remains a matter of law, but the AI Act's boundaries depend on this distinction.
