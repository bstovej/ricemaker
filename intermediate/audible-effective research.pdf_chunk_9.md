## 🔎 I. Case Study Methodology

**Core Insight:** A case study is valuable when the goal is to **deeply describe, explore, or explain a single or small set of cases**, rather than generalizing findings to a wider population.

### Key Uses for Case Studies:
*   **Deep Understanding:** Provides context and meaning, especially useful for descriptive or historical research.
*   **Hypothesis Generation:** Used as initial research to generate testable hypotheses when existing literature is sparse or theory is untested.
*   **Plausibility Probes:** Acts as a pilot study to test the viability of an argument or method, particularly useful when seeking funding (proof of concept).

### Practical Facts & Warnings:
*   **Data Richness:** Benefits from using **multiple types and sources of data** (observation, interviews, focus groups, surveys, archival documents).
*   **Sampling:** Random selection is not usually applicable. **Purposive sampling** is common, meaning cases are selected based on their relevance to the variables of interest.
*   **Ethical Warning (Causation):** Caution is advised because the case study setting can lead to a bias where the case is "built to pass the test," making it an inappropriate test for the argument itself.
*   **Variation is Key:** Comparative case studies require variation in variables (both independent and dependent) to allow researchers to make claims about correlation or causation.

### Robert Yin’s Five Categories of Cases:
| Category | Definition | Purpose |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Critical Case** | Investigates a specific application of an existing theory. | The theory *must* explain this case for the theory to gain wide acceptance (e.g., explaining the French Revolution). |
| **Extreme Case** | Examines deviant or outlier cases. | Used to understand anomalies that fall far outside the expected typical pattern. |
| **Representative Case** | Looks at a typical, average case of a phenomenon. | Used to illustrate a general pattern; based on cases close to the mean (e.g., low z-score). |
| **Revelatory Case** | A case that was previously unstudied but is newly available for analysis. | Allows the researcher to provide useful new information using special or new access. |
| **Longitudinal Case** | Explores a single case over time (at least two points). | Allows for the examination of change and evolution. |

## 🌍 II. Interpretivism vs. Positivism (Philosophical Approaches)

**Core Insight:** These are competing worldviews regarding the nature of reality, which dictates the research design.

### Positivism (The "Objective Reality" View):
*   **Assumption:** There is an objective, measurable reality that can be studied using systematic designs to determine a universal "truth."
*   **Approach:** Highly focused on testing theories and hypotheses (deductive approach).

### Interpretivism (The "Subjective Reality" View):
*   **Assumption:** There is no single, objective truth. Reality is subjective, open to multiple interpretations, and composed of multiple layers of meaning.
*   **Approach:** Focuses on **understanding and interpreting** rather than testing. It uses an **inductive process**—building theory from collected data and observations, rather than testing pre-existing ideas.

## 🏞️ III. Field Research & Ethnography

**Definition:** Field research is an overall approach where data must be acquired locally from the subjects' natural environment ("going into the field").

*   **Ethnography:** A specific type of field research that involves **embedding oneself** in the community under study (participant observation). It focuses on understanding a culture or group, rather than just explaining an outcome.

### Practical Considerations for Field Research:
*   **Ethical Approval:** Mandatory to get approval from an ethical review board, respecting both the researcher's institution and the host country's standards.
*   **Intercultural Competence:** Requires the researcher to be respectful, responsive, and critically aware of their own cultural biases.
*   **Planning:** Requires careful planning regarding the **duration** (funding/grants are essential) and the **timing** (respecting local seasons, holidays, and events).

## 🗣️ IV. Interviews

**Purpose:** A primary method for collecting qualitative data; ideal for learning about motivations, gaining subjective understandings, and exploring individual experiences.

### Key Challenges & Techniques:
*   **Access:** The biggest barrier; gaining the time and willingness of busy subjects is difficult.
    *   **Solution:** **Snowball sampling** is often used, where initial subjects introduce the researcher to others.
    *   **Caveat:** Snowball sampling can lead to a biased, unrepresentative sample (people tend to refer friends/allies).
*   **Bias & Truth:** Researchers must guard against their own assumptions and remember that subjects may not give a representative perspective (they might lie or only tell the researcher the "story" they expect). Triangulation (comparing sources) is recommended to verify truth.

### Data Collection Recording Methods:
The method chosen influences the quality of the data:
1.  **Recording (Audio/Video):** Best record accuracy, but risks **reactivity bias** (subjects may change their answers knowing they are being recorded).
2.  **Taking Notes:** Avoids the reactivity issue but can inhibit rapport-building and may lead to missing details or inaccurate quotes.
3.  **Notes After the Fact:** Avoids the distraction of real-time note-taking, but is the least accurate, as it forces subjective judgment on what was "important" and risks losing data.

### Interview Question Styles (Structure):
The questions can be structured, semi-structured, or unstructured (though the text ends before detailing the differences).
