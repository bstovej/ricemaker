### **Key Insights**

*   **The "Security Tool Paradox":** The most striking finding is that the use of security tools (physical guardianship) was **positively correlated** with the probability of malware infection. Contrary to the researchers' hypothesis (H3), the data suggested that having security tools installed was associated with an *increase* in the likelihood of being infected.
*   **The Efficacy of Personal Guardianship:** Unlike tools, security **activities** (personal guardianship)—such as setting passwords, updating operating systems, and checking security settings—were consistently associated with a **decrease** in malware infection and risk levels.
*   **Risk-Based Behavior as a Predictor:** The study confirms that the type of online activity is a direct predictor of malware presence. "More risky" online activities significantly increased the log odds of infection, while "less risky" activities showed no correlation with infection rates.
*   **The Limitation of Automation:** While security tools excel at automating the detection of *known* threats, they struggle with "zero-day" or previously unseen threats. In contrast, human-led security activities are more adaptable to emerging risks, though they are vulnerable to human error (e.g., clicking phishing links).
*   **The Complexity of Domestic Security:** The researchers highlight a "monitoring gap." While security activities can be effectively monitored and sanctioned in an organizational setting, they are harder to enforce in a home setting, making the individual's behavior the primary line of defense.
