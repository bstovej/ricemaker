This document is a segment of an LL.M. thesis from Tilburg Law School (dated February 2025) titled *"Meta’s ‘Consent-or-Pay’ after the European Data Protection Board Opinion 08/2024: Is GDPR Compliance Possible?"*

The paper analyzes the legality of Meta’s subscription model, where users must choose between paying a monthly fee or consenting to personalized advertising, through the lens of the GDPR and recent regulatory opinions.

### **Key Facts**
*   **The "Consent-or-Pay" Model:** In November 2023, Meta introduced a model for Facebook and Instagram users in the EU, EEA, and Switzerland. Users are presented with two choices:
    1.  **Paid Subscription:** €12.99/month for an ad-free experience where data is not used for personalized ads.
    2.  **Free Access:** Continued use of the platforms in exchange for consenting to personal data processing for behavioral advertising.
*   **Regulatory Context:** The model follows a period of "rumors" and has triggered significant legal challenges from NGOs (like **NOYB**) and consumer protection groups (like **BECT**), who argue that financial pressure renders consent invalid.
*   **The EDPB’s Position (Opinion 08/2024):** The European Data Protection Board (EDPB) has proposed that a "third" option is necessary for GDPR compliance: a **"Free Alternative Without Behavioural Advertising" (FAWBA)**. This would involve a free tier using only contextual or general advertising, rather than personalized tracking.
*   **The CJEU Position:** The Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU), in the *Meta v Bundeskartellamt* case, suggested that an ad-free option with an "appropriate fee" is permissible.

### **Key Insights & Legal Arguments**

#### **1. The Central Legal Conflict: EDPB vs. CJEU**
There is a significant tension between the EDPB's recent opinion and the CJEU’s previous ruling. 
*   The **EDPB** argues that "consent-or-pay" models may be coercive and that users need a third, non-tracking free option to ensure consent is "freely given."
*   The **Author** argues that the EDPB's stance may constitute "legal overreach" and contradicts the CJEU’s allowance of an "appropriate fee."

#### **2. The "Commodification of Data" Debate**
A major point of contention is whether personal data can be treated as a "tradeable commodity."
*   **The EDPB's view:** Suggests that using a fee to avoid data processing effectively turns a fundamental right (privacy) into a luxury for the wealthy and treats data as a commodity.
*   **The Author's rebuttal:** Argues that the EDPB is technically incorrect. The author cites the **EU Consumer Rights Omnibus Directive** and the **Digital Content Directive**, which recognize that personal data can legally serve as an alternative to money for a service.

#### **3. The "Waiver of Rights" Misconception**
The author disputes the EDPB's implication that users "waive" their GDPR rights once they consent to behavioral advertising. The author asserts that even when a user chooses the "free" (data-processing) version:
*   The controller (Meta) remains fully responsible for GDPR compliance.
*   The user retains rights (transparency, access, etc.).
*   The "consent" does not strip the user of their fundamental protections under the GDPR.

#### **4. The Concept of "Consent" and Economic Balance**
The document highlights a clash between two legal interpretations:
*   **The Pro-Regulatory View (EDPB):** Requires a third option to prevent "coerced" consent through the threat of a paywall.
*   **The Pro-Market/Platform View (Thesis):** Argues that a balance must be struck between the right to privacy and the economic necessity of platforms to fund services via data-driven advertising, provided the choice is presented fairly.

### **Summary of the Author's Thesis Position**
The author appears to be critiquing the EDPB's stance, arguing that the "third option" requirement might be an overreach that ignores the reality of the digital economy and the fact that users do not lose their legal rights simply by opting into a data-driven business model.
